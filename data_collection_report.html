SECTION 1Summary

The paper's published corpus is 20 newspaper articles / 250 sentences, drawn from a single outlet (The Hindu) in a single country. The authors state their next objective as extending coverage to "at least ten countries in the Global South."

This report assesses one candidate source already held, and documents a wider collection assembled to support that extension.

2.2  Is it the right kind of data?

Yes — and it is arguably a stronger choice than the direct newspaper scraping used in the published work.

  • It is newspaper data, the source type the ontology is designed around. A single bulletin carries ~70 articles from The Hindu, The Times of India, The Indian Express, The Tribune, Dainik Bhaskar, Amar Ujala and many regional outlets.
  • Provenance is citable and stable. Each bulletin has a permanent, dated government URL, and each article within it prints its own original publisher URL — materially better for reproducibility than ad-hoc scraping.
  • Selection is made by the national drug-control authority, not the researcher, removing one source of investigator bias in corpus construction.
  • It is already multi-country. The single held bulletin contains incidents from Nepal, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka alongside India — directly serving the paper's stated goal.
  • Entity density matches the ontology — Drug, Person/Trafficker, Arrest, Seizure, Quantity, Value, Location, Date, Law Enforcement Agency, Transport, Legal Action, Asset.
  • The editorial sections are effectively free requirements analysis. Each bulletin's Emerging Modus Operandi and Key Trends sections describe trafficking methods in structured prose — drone delivery across the Amritsar border, ambulances used to evade checkpoints, cocaine moved as pharmaceutical consignments, cannabis intercropped with maize and cotton.

3.1  Extraction method and reliability

Two layers were produced, of differing reliability, and the distinction should be preserved downstream:

  • Article bodies (reliable). Read from page text; each carries its original publisher URL. Recommended for text mining.
  • Index metadata (less reliable). The bulletins' NEWS INDEX tables are the only source of region and publisher attribution. Recovered from PDF table geometry, because linear text extraction merges the Publisher and Headline columns irrecoverably. 77 of 3,286 index rows (2.3%) are flagged in the output as having a questionable publisher cell.
Why this is documented

The bulletins are not typeset consistently — column positions, headline chunking (per-headline vs. per-word), script (Latin vs. Devanagari) and even vertical text direction vary between issues. A naïve parser fails silently: an early fixed-threshold version produced 1,437 spurious "publishers" from a corpus containing a few hundred real outlets. A separate defect truncated the source URLs of 641 articles (19%); this was corrected, and all 3,338 now carry complete, citable URLs.

A methodological caution

Colombia alone contributes approximately 50% of the global dataset, because it reports at municipality granularity while most states report only major cases. Cross-country comparisons in IDS therefore measure national reporting practice at least as much as trafficking activity.

And a positive justification for this work

UNODC holds 92 seizure records for Bangladesh and none whatsoever for Nepal. A single NCB bulletin, by contrast, carries Nepali and Bangladeshi trafficking incidents in full narrative detail, with named actors, quantities, routes and outcomes.

The two sources are complementary rather than substitutable: UNODC supplies structure and volume where states report well; the newspaper corpus supplies coverage where they do not. This is a principled, evidence-based argument for the newspaper-driven approach in exactly the region the paper targets — and is worth stating explicitly in any extension of the work.

SECTION 6Proposed ontology extensions

The collected data evidences several classes absent from the published ontology (40 classes, 21 object properties). Those marked below can be populated from official UNODC value lists rather than derived ad hoc from text.

Aligning to the UNODC vocabularies would also make the ontology interoperable with the principal international reporting instrument in this domain. A further opportunity: India's NIDAAN (National Integrated Database on Arrested Narco-offenders) publishes its official field schema in its user manual. The microdata is restricted, but aligning DTO to that schema would mean the ontology speaks the Indian government's own data model.

SECTION 7Data-quality issues encountered

Recorded because they affect reproducibility and would otherwise cause silent data loss.

The NCB server truncates large downloads without error

On first collection, 14 of 117 PDFs arrived incomplete while returning success. File sizes were plausible and only opening each file revealed the problem — one annual report downloaded as 196 KB against a true size of 101 MB; another as 25 MB against 60 MB. All retrieval was re-implemented to verify against Content-Length and to open each PDF, retrying with HTTP resume. Any independent collection from this source should do likewise.

UNODC IDS workbooks are not internally consistent

The annual worksheets do not share a column order (2011–2017 and 2018+ differ), the 2023 sheet's header is misspelled, and the transport column is named Trafficking Mode of Transportation rather than …Transport. Reading by column position, or assuming the header name, silently corrupts the data — an initial pass produced a transport column that was 100% empty and resembled missing source data rather than a defect. The data has been normalised into a single verified file with these variants handled explicitly.

Additionally, published download links on the UNODC platform point to an internal hostname path; the apparently correct public path returns an HTML error page carrying an .xlsx filename.

SECTION 8Limitations of this collection

Stated plainly for completeness.

  • The GDELT harvest is incomplete. A supplementary harvest of newspaper metadata across 18 Global South countries was attempted via the GDELT DOC 2.0 API. GDELT rate-limited the host and continued to refuse requests even at 12-second spacing; collection was stopped rather than persist against a free public service. 416 article records for India only were obtained, and the status is recorded as PARTIAL in the output manifest. For complete coverage, GDELT's BigQuery tables should be used instead of the public API.
  • Pakistan has no machine-readable national statistics. The Anti-Narcotics Force publishes only news images and press releases under obfuscated paths. Pakistan is covered instead by the 17,935 UNODC IDS records.
  • Nepal has no public statistical publication. Nepali incidents are available only through the newspaper corpus; UNODC holds no records at all.
  • NCRB Crime in India is unavailable for 2023 and 2024. Those years appear in the site's year selector but return no tables — they are not yet published.
  • One NCB bulletin (2026-09-05) is image-only and requires OCR; older NCB annual reports are scanned documents and likewise require OCR for text analysis.

SECTION 9Suggested next steps

  1. Continue periodic collection of NCB bulletins. The archive is a rolling ~3-month window; bulletins not captured are permanently lost. Sustained collection is the only route to a corpus with temporal depth.
  2. Use UNODC IDS as an evaluation set for extracted Seizure instances — it provides officially attested ground truth at event level.
  3. Align Drug, TransportMode and SeizureLocationType to the UNODC vocabularies rather than deriving them from article text.
  4. Extend the ontology with the classes in Section 6, which the collected data already evidences.
  5. If publishing a derived dataset, release annotations and source URLs, not article text, to respect newspaper copyright. Every article in the corpus carries its original URL, so this is straightforward.
